# Writing Review: The Vocabulary of Weather in English-Language Novels by Author Birth Cohort, 1770–1899

## Summary assessment

The draft is clear, honest about its limits and mostly well hedged. The researcher's corrections (fog, snow, storm, realised birth years) are applied consistently. Most arithmetic I recomputed from the stated counts holds. Examples are the pooled rates, the log ratios for tempest, sunlight, fog, mist, snow and misty, and the precision counts (140/198, and 22+21+21+10+12+20+20+14 = 140).

Five problems remain. They are mostly problems of alignment between headline claims and the paper's own evidence, plus a few definitional inconsistencies.

1. The headline framing ("stable total, movement beneath it") contradicts the paper's own admission that most family-level movement is not distinguishable from noise.
2. The abstract has a garbled core sentence, is too long, and repeats the same four numbers in four places.
3. The hypotheses set up in the literature section are never revisited, and one is contradicted by a result that goes unremarked.
4. Several lexicon terms (ambiguous, core, extended, full, family) are defined differently in different sections.
5. There is no discussion section, and the conclusion largely restates the abstract.

## 1. Structure and argument flow (pass 1: topic sentences)

**Reading only the first sentences gives a coherent story**, but the story is the weaker one. The intro and abstract say "stable total, families move". Section 4 says "total not distinguishable from stable, families mostly not distinguishable from noise, one suggestive snow rise".

**1.1 Stability versus movement (high priority).**
- The abstract says "Beneath this stability, individual word families move". The introduction says "the stability of the total conceals movement in individual word families". Finding 4 then says "most of the family-level trends we report are not distinguishable from zero".
- "Conceals" asserts that movement exists. Only snow and ice reaches p < 0.05, and it is non-monotone (0.82, then 2.06, then 1.44). The paper itself expects about 0.75 false positives from 15 profiles, and it reports two.
- "Stable" and "remarkably stable" also overclaim. The cohort 2 interval is 7.01–15.32 and cohort 4's is 10.87–15.71. With 14 novels per cohort, the design can detect only large differences, as the paper says. The accurate statement is "no detectable difference in the composite rate". Absence of evidence is not evidence of stability.
- Suggested rewrite for the abstract: "The pooled weather rate shows no detectable difference across cohorts (11–13 hits per 10,000 tokens, with wide author-level intervals). Word families differ in profile, but few differences are distinguishable from noise."
- Apply the same change in the introduction ("remarkably stable"), in Section 4.1 and in the conclusion.

**1.2 Literature expectations are never tested or revisited.**
- Section 2 says the fog literature "motivate[s] the expectation that fog vocabulary should be prominent in cohorts writing during the era of coal smoke" and "treats the expectation as a hypothesis".
- Section 4 never returns to it. The most relevant result is that cohort 2 (born 1803–1819, writing in the early to mid-Victorian decades) has a near-absence of fog (9 occurrences, G² = 61.59, log ratio −2.83). This sits directly against the expectation and goes unremarked.
- The literature review also lists three expectations (Romantic storm and cloud, mid-Victorian fog, modernist retreat from set-piece weather). The tempest, gale and clouds result for cohort 1 bears on the first. Nothing is said about the other two.
- The paper has no discussion section. Section 6 is limitations and Section 7 is the conclusion. Add a short discussion, about 300 words, that states for each expectation whether the data support it, contradict it or cannot speak to it (given precision of 0.50 for fog).
- Hanlon (2018) is introduced as "the measurable counterpart" but never used. Either compare descriptively or cut the sentence. Hanlon's series is London-specific and the corpus is mostly not London fiction.

**1.3 The conclusion duplicates the abstract.**
- The snow figures (0.82, 2.06, 1.44) appear verbatim in the abstract, the introduction, Section 4.2 and the conclusion. The fog "0 of 4" sentence appears in four places.
- Vary the role of each: the abstract carries the numbers, the introduction carries the claims, the results carry the evidence, and the conclusion states what the reader should take away.
- The paragraph beginning "Our work has implications for practice" is generic. Its content is the useful point (validate dictionary counts by hand, and expect profiles to change with ambiguous forms). State it in one sentence without the "implications" framing.

**1.4 Introduction roadmap** matches the section order. No change needed. The fourth sentence of the contributions paragraph is long; split it.

## 2. Evidence–claim alignment (pass 2)

**2.1 Tempest as a cohort-1 "marker" (high priority).**
- The abstract, introduction and conclusion state that "tempest is concentrated in / belongs to the earliest cohort". Section 4.3 says keyness lists are "candidates for reading and not established cohort markers".
- The storm family has precision of 0.42. Tempest was not validated at term level, and it could be the title or quoted text of Shakespeare's play. Single novels contribute many occurrences of rare terms (the paper says so itself).
- Hedge it in the abstract and conclusion ("tempest is more frequent in the earliest cohort, a keyness result not checked at term level or by author"). Alternatively report how many of the 75 occurrences come from the top novel or author, which the dispersion data should allow.

**2.2 Fog rise: "0 of 4".**
- The hand check supports "does not support reading the rise as more fog description". The sample behind the strongest wording is four items, and the upper 95% bound for 0/4 is about 60%.
- Say "n = 4" wherever the sentence appears, and avoid "cannot be read" without the small-n qualifier. The abstract does mention four, which is good. The introduction and conclusion should do the same.
- The post-hoc result is described as "weakens the rise … no longer distinguishable from no drift". The original profile (Spearman 0.77, p = 0.072) was not distinguishable either. What actually changes is rho (0.77 to 0.54) and the rate (1.53 to 1.26). The cohort-1 to cohort-6 ratio is still about 1.9 (0.65 to 1.26). Rewrite as "attenuates" and give the ratio. Do not imply the p-value crossed a threshold.

**2.3 Post-hoc storm decline p = 0.005.**
- The paper calls it "a pattern worth testing, not a finding". That is the right label.
- Reporting p = 0.005 beside it gives a number that readers will quote. The list was chosen after looking at the sample, many lexicon variants were examined, and the reduced family was not re-validated. Either drop the p-value from the abstract-level discussion, or add "not adjusted for the lexicon variants examined".
- The 15-profile multiplicity count in Section 4.2 excludes all robustness profiles. Say so.

**2.4 Leave-out claims.**
- "Leave-one-author-out rates show that no single author reverses a cohort's position" sits beside the heaviest-novel result, where removing one novel per cohort drops the trend from rho 0.60 to 0.09 and "removes most of the bump". The two statements pull in different directions, and the first has no stated criterion.
- The cohort 1 range (9.42–12.29) spans more than the whole between-cohort range of 10.81–13.32. "No author reverses a position" cannot be read off ranges alone. Report the rank order under leave-out, or drop the sentence.
- "Excluding capitalised mid-sentence hits … leaves the picture unchanged, with a Spearman correlation of 0.31 for the extended lexicon". The unfiltered extended-lexicon value is 0.60, so 0.31 is not "unchanged". Give both values.

**2.5 "Differences between authors are larger than differences between cohorts."**
- The evidence is the author range (2.10–31.35) against the cohort range. These are not comparable: a range over about 69 units against a range over six means. Use a variance decomposition, for example within-cohort author SD against the SD of cohort rates, or the share of variance between cohorts.
- Delete "every one of the 84 novels contains at least one weather word". At about 11 per 10,000 tokens and at least 30,000 tokens per novel, this is nearly guaranteed and uninformative.

**2.6 Unambiguous lexicon "shows a rising profile" (4.43, then 6.57, then 5.60; rho 0.71, p = 0.111).**
- This is a peak at cohort 4 and a fall afterwards, with p = 0.11. Section 5.2 then says "the full dictionary is flat, the core lexicon is flat, and the unambiguous lexicon rises".
- The earlier sentence calls the core lexicon "flat to slightly rising … and back to 8.03", which is below the cohort-1 value of 8.36. Use one description per variant. "Peaks in cohort 4 in all three variants" is the accurate shared feature, with the caveat that removing the heaviest novel removes the full-dictionary peak.

**2.7 Keyness interpretation.**
- "The earliest cohort thus favours the vocabulary of the sky in motion … and the later cohorts favour the vocabulary of light" generalises from sunlight and sunshine. Cohort 3 has no key terms, and cohort 6 has only *misty*. Clouds (precision 0.58) and gale are part of the storm and cloud families whose precision is below 0.6.
- Limit the sentence to "cohort 1 uses *tempest*, *gale* and *clouds* more, and *sunlight* and *sunshine* less, than the other cohorts".
- "Mist and its relatives are more frequent in cohorts 4 and 6; this is the keyness counterpart of the family profiles above." The cohort-6 fog family was the one with 0 of 4 correct, and *misty* was not hand-checked. State that.

**2.8 Collocates paragraph.**
- It reports no PMI values and no counts except two. "The storm came, passed or tossed a ship" is unsupported by any table. Ranking by PMI with a minimum count of five favours rare pairs. "Consistent with the high precision we find for rain and wind families" is a leap, because no wind pair is named.
- Add a small table (pair, count, PMI) or shorten the paragraph to one sentence. Note that *thunder storm*, the only storm pair named, contains a form the post-hoc analysis judged unusable.

**2.9 Precision claims.**
- "Precision of the dictionary is 0.71 overall" (abstract) is a pooled figure over a design-based sample. It combines a composite sample with seven family samples that overlap in the underlying hits, and the sample is not weighted by term frequency (Section 3.3 says so). It is not a corpus precision. Call it "pooled sample accuracy" or drop it from the abstract and lead with the family-level figures.
- Give Wilson intervals. For 21/24 the interval is roughly 0.69–0.96, so "a good measure" for precipitation, wind, snow and sun rests on small samples. The 24-item samples cannot separate 0.83 from 0.88.
- "Wind … is a good measure" sits oddly with the fact that *wind* is listed as ambiguous (verb) in the design and dropped in the unambiguous lexicon.

**2.10 Missing confound in the interpretation.** The plan listed genre as a main confound. The paper's own corpus shows cohort-specific genre clusters that plausibly drive the family profiles (sea and Gothic fiction in the early cohorts; Westerns, adventure and outdoor fiction in cohorts 5 and 6). The limitations section mentions only digitisation and popularity. Add genre composition (unmeasured) as a limitation and as an alternative reading of the tempest, storm, snow and sun patterns.

**2.11 Contribution claims.**
- "A validation template" overstates. The validation has one judge, no stated rubric, no agreement statistic and 24 items per family. The plan promised a second coder and κ. If those were not done, say so in Methods; if the validation was by a single judge, state that and drop "template".
- "We found no study that compares weather vocabulary across author-birth cohorts; this statement rests on a limited literature search and should be read as such." The literature review itself lists Moretti, Jockers, Heuser and Le-Khac, Underwood and the Stanford Literary Lab as unsearched. Run those searches, or move the caveat out of the body text into a limitation. A reader should not find "should be read as such" in the related-literature section.
- The claim that existing accounts "rest on a small number of canonical texts" is made about Waugh (2008). The literature review says the Waugh summary comes "from the title only". Check the content before characterising it.

## 3. Definitions and consistency

- **"Ambiguous" forms.** Section 3.2 lists shower(s), thunder, icy and cloud forms. Section 5.2 says 17 forms are ambiguous, "among them wind, storm, sun, cloud and thunder forms", and the data dictionary adds Snow, Rain and Frost surnames. Give one list, in an appendix or footnote.
- **Core lexicon.** The paper says 23 forms "built from the focal terms named in the research question and their plurals". Seven focal terms and their plurals are 14 forms. The specification says the core is the twelve headline words including mist, thunder, frost, breeze and sunshine. Reconcile and list the 23 forms.
- **Dictionary names.** "Dictionary", "lexicon", "extended lexicon", "full dictionary" and "composite" all appear. "Extended lexicon" is used in the capitalised-hits sentence without definition; the specification defines it as the full 90-form list. Choose "full dictionary" and "core lexicon" and use them everywhere.
- **Family names.** "Fog words", "fog and mist words", "fog family" and "fog" all appear. "Snow and ice words" and "snow-related" also appear. The family names in tables, text and abstract should match. Use "fog and mist" throughout.
- **Hits, words, tokens.** The introduction says "every cohort uses between about 11 and 13 weather words per 10,000 tokens". The measure is dictionary hits (tokens), not distinct words. Use "hits" consistently.
- **Cohort 1 width.** The data dictionary says the paper states that cohort 1 spans 30 years and the others 20. The draft does not say so. Add one sentence in Section 3.1.
- **Realised ranges.** The title and abstract use 1770–1899 and 1880–1899. The realised maximum is 1890. The abstract should state "born 1770–1890" once, as instruction 7 required. The title may keep the design ranges.
- **Reason for the sample size.** "The final corpus is smaller because the exclusions were applied after the texts were loaded and word counts became known" is incomplete. The data summary shows the binding constraint was cohort 2 running out of eligible novels at 14, after translation and non-novel exclusions. Also, "the preceding design" is opaque; say "the plan called for about 120 novels (20 per cohort)".
- **Numerals.** "None of the 4 sampled fog hits" (results) against "none of four" (abstract and conclusion). Spell out numbers below ten in running text. "A plateau near 3" should be a value or a range. "P value" is typeset inconsistently ("$p$ value" and "p value").
- **Tense.** Mixed past and present in related literature ("We found no study" next to "We follow this advice"). Use the past for what you did, the present for what the paper shows.
- **Informal register.** "Bump" appears twice in 4.1; use "peak" or "higher rate". "Staple of the English novel" and "rich account" in the opening are loose.

## 4. Clarity and sentence level (pass 3)

**Abstract**
- The sentence "we count a hand-built dictionary of 90 exact word forms per 10,000 tokens" reads as if the forms are counted per 10,000 tokens. Rewrite: "we count hits of a hand-built dictionary of 90 exact word forms and report rates per 10,000 tokens."
- The abstract is about 300 words and carries about 14 numbers. Target 200–220 words. Keep: corpus size, composite range, snow and ice, tempest, the fog caveat, the precision figures for storm, fog and cloud, and the one-clause post-hoc storm result.
- The opening claim ("accounts typically rest on a few canonical texts") is unsupported in the abstract. Reduce it to "Existing accounts of weather in the Victorian novel are largely close readings".
- Move the parenthetical "a pattern we regard as suggestive" so that it follows the claim without breaking the number sequence.

**Section 3**
- "The sampling frame is the set of the 320 most popular catalogue rows per cohort search, sorted by download count" is clear. The following sentences (4,160 rows, 1,409, 632) read better as a short table of sample flow, which exists in the data summary. A numbered flow table here would replace five % src lines and one long sentence.
- The sentence beginning "For each of the eight measures … we drew a cohort-stratified sample … with ambiguous terms placed first within each cohort cell" is unclear. Does "placed first" mean oversampled? What was the rule when a cell had fewer hits? Who judged, and were they blind to cohort? Rewrite as two or three sentences.
- "Keyness treats tokens as independent, which overstates certainty; we therefore use it to rank terms and read the author-level intervals alongside it." No author-level keyness check is reported in the results. State what was done or cut the clause.

**Section 4**
- Section 4.2's first block is a single paragraph of nine or more sentences carrying about 25 numbers. Split into (a) snow and ice, (b) fog, storm and cloud, and (c) precipitation, wind and sun. Put the profiles in the table and keep only the interpretive sentences in the text.
- The sentence "Precipitation words peak in cohort 5 at 1.50 and are lowest in cohort 1 at 0.74, wind words show no ordered drift (Spearman −0.09), and sun words rise from 1.98 in cohort 1 to a plateau near 3 in cohorts 3 to 5 before falling to 2.25 in cohort 6" mixes three claims. Split it.
- Section 4.3 is similar: about a dozen G² and log-ratio values in running prose. Use a table with columns for term, counts, G² and log ratio, and keep the interpretation in the text.
- "Although the reference is five times as large" is awkward. Give the rates per 10,000 tokens instead.
- "Numbers need baselines": rates per 10,000 tokens have no concrete anchor. A 100,000-token novel (about the median) at 11.4 hits contains about 114 weather hits. Snow and ice at 0.82 and 2.06 are about 8 and 21 hits per such novel. One such translation in 4.2 helps the reader judge "large".
- Figure 1 caption: the nested parentheses make it hard to read. Put the cohort-to-birth-year mapping in a sentence after the caption's first sentence.

**Slop and banned-phrase check**
- "Our research makes three contributions" is close to the banned "this paper contributes to". The substance is stated, which is fine, but contribution 3 (template) is overclaimed, as above.
- "Remarkably stable" and "good measure" are vague; replace with the numbers.
- "Future research could pursue two specific questions" is acceptable because the two questions are specific.

**Passive and nominalisation.** Passive voice is mostly confined to methods ("are removed", "is applied") and is acceptable. "A hand-judged sample", "the validation judged unusable" are fine. One cluster to tighten: "the 48,466 characters removed across the corpus are recorded per document" (state in the data appendix instead).

## 5. Minor points

- Several `% src:` and `% computed:` comments are fine for the pipeline, but strip them from the submission copy.
- The precision-by-cohort figures (28/33 and 18/33) have a `% computed` comment only. If the per-cohort figures are cited, show them in a table beside the family precision.
- Dropping the heaviest novel is described as "the single heaviest novel of each cohort". Name the novels or authors in the supplement. Readers will want to know whether the cohort 4 bump comes from, for example, one adventure novel.
- Define "hits" at first use in the abstract, and define "family" in the methods before using it in results.
- The related-literature paragraph on the pathetic fallacy ends by promising to "qualify every cohort contrast in a family where figurative use is common". Section 4 does this only for fog. Do it for storm and cloud too, or soften the promise.

## 6. Priority fixes

1. Rework the abstract: fix the garbled counting sentence, cut to about 210 words, replace "stable / beneath this stability … move" with "no detectable difference / few differences distinguishable from noise".
2. Align the stability and movement language in the introduction, Section 4 and the conclusion with the paper's own power caveat.
3. Hedge tempest as an unvalidated keyness result; add the top-novel or author share.
4. Add a short discussion section that returns to the three literature expectations and notes that cohort 2's near-absence of fog contradicts the coal-smoke expectation.
5. Unify the lexicon definitions (ambiguous list, core 23 forms, dictionary and lexicon names, family names).
6. Correct the "unchanged" (0.60 to 0.31) and "no author reverses a position" statements; describe each lexicon variant once and accurately.
7. Describe the hand-check procedure (judge, blinding, rubric, ordering rule, sample overlap), add intervals, and retire "validation template" and the pooled "0.71 overall" in the abstract.
8. Move dense numbers from the 4.2 and 4.3 paragraphs into tables, and add a per-novel baseline for the rates.
9. Add genre composition as a limitation and an alternative reading; run the missing distant-reading searches or relocate the caveat.

OVERALL SCORE: 6.5/10
RECOMMENDATION: Minor Revision
