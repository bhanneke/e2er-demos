# Referee Report (Literature Dimension): Monthly 2 m Air Temperature in Frankfurt am Main (NASA POWER), Trend and 36-Month Forecast Skill

## Summary of the submission

The paper describes the 1981-2022 trend in a MERRA-2 grid-cell series (NASA POWER) for Frankfurt. It estimates about +0.50 °C per decade. It then compares ETS(A,N,A), ETS(A,Ad,A), SARIMA and a trend-plus-harmonics regression with seasonal naive and naive baselines on a frozen 36-month hold-out (2023-2025). RMSE falls by about 30% relative to seasonal naive, with Diebold-Mariano p-values between 0.009 and 0.037. The authors acknowledge the contribution is modest. My assessment concentrates on literature coverage and positioning, with comments on the other dimensions where the literature bears on them.

DIMENSION SCORES:
- Contribution: 4/10
- Identification: 6/10 (no causal claim; descriptive and predictive design is appropriate)
- Empirics: 5.5/10
- Writing: 6/10
- Literature: 3.5/10

## MAJOR CONCERNS

1. **The paper cites no climate, reanalysis or regional-warming literature.** All 13 citations are statistical-methods sources or the data portal. The introduction and discussion assert that Central European temperature is warming, that anomalies are about 2 °C, that Frankfurt is an urban heat island, and that practitioners in heat management, energy and agriculture use seasonal-naive heuristics. None of these claims has a reference. The literature scan found the relevant sources: Kaspar et al. (2013) and the DWD *Nationaler Klimareport* for the station network and national trends; the IPCC AR6 WGI Europe chapter; Rousi et al. (2022) on western European heatwave trends; and Hersbach et al. (2020) for ERA5. These are not cited in the draft, apparently because they were never registered in the bib. The result is a paper that reports a +0.50 °C/decade trend with no external benchmark. The H1 prior of 0.3-0.5 °C/decade is "from regional literature" in the plan, but the manuscript names no source. A reader cannot judge whether 0.50 °C/decade is plausible, high or low for Hesse or Germany. *Path to revision:* register and cite the DWD/Kaspar, IPCC and ERA5 sources, and compare the trend with published DWD figures for Germany or Hesse after 1981.

2. **The data product is not situated in the reanalysis literature, and the main validity threat is unaddressed.** The text cites only the POWER portal. It does not cite MERRA-2 (Gelaro et al. 2017) or the literature on reanalysis trend inhomogeneity caused by changes in the assimilated observing system, which is the standard objection to using reanalysis for trend estimation. The plan listed this as a data_reviewer item and as a sensitivity analysis (estimate models from 1991 onward), but the manuscript neither discusses it nor runs it. Equally missing is evidence on MERRA-2 and POWER T2M skill over Central Europe or in urban cells. The literature scan found no Germany-specific POWER validation, and the draft should say so openly. Without this, the headline trend and the "warm hold-out" interpretation rest on an unvalidated series. The paper handles the station-versus-grid issue only as a scope condition. *Path to revision:* cite MERRA-2 and reanalysis-trend studies, add a short paragraph on known inhomogeneities, and either run the post-1991 sensitivity or state clearly that it was not done. A cross-check with ERA5 (public) would be a feasible substitute for the dropped DWD comparison, since the researcher's decision only excludes DWD data.

3. **The forecasting literature that frames the benchmark is missing, and the benchmark choice is weak against that literature.** The paper cites Hyndman and Athanasopoulos and the standard accuracy and test sources, but not the following:
   - Hyndman et al. (2002), the ETS state-space framework. The paper fits ETS models and cites only the auto.arima paper, which is not the ETS reference.
   - The M3 and M4 results (Makridakis and Hibon 2000; Makridakis et al. 2020), which are the usual motivation for comparing simple methods.
   - Forecast combination.
   - Climate and statistical forecasting at long horizons: Doblas-Reyes et al. (2013) on seasonal prediction skill, and decadal prediction (Smith et al. 2019; Boer et al. 2016). The plan listed these as the contrast class for "statistical baselines at 36 months". The draft never mentions dynamical forecasts, so the stated practitioner motivation (a 3-year horizon) is not tied to what is otherwise available.

   More substantively, the literature scan and the plan both call for a seasonal-mean (climatology) benchmark, noting that seasonal naive is a weak benchmark for noisy seasonal series because it carries one year of anomaly noise. The draft has no climatology benchmark and no combination. Hence the headline "30% gain over seasonal naive" mostly measures the cost of using a single noisy year, and cannot be read as evidence that modelling the trend matters. This weakens the contribution as framed in the introduction ("quantifies the forecast gain of simple statistical models"). The "~30%" figure is expected from theory (roughly twice the error variance of a climatological mean) and should be positioned as such. *Path to revision:* add a calendar-month climatology (full-training and last-30-year) as a baseline, and cite the literature explaining why seasonal naive is weak here.

4. **Forecast-comparison inference is not positioned against its known limitations.** The paper cites Diebold-Mariano and the HLN correction, but not the literature on when DM applies: Diebold (2015) and Giacomini and White (2006) on forecasts versus models and conditional predictive ability, and Clark and West (2007) on nested models. The harmonic regression nests a seasonal-mean model, and the ETS(A,N,A) model nests simple seasonal level models. The plan flagged the Clark-West caveat, but the draft does not mention it. In addition, the 36 errors come from one origin, so they are serially dependent multi-horizon errors from a single forecast path, and a "DM statistic with h = 12" is applied to them without a stated justification. The rolling-origin exercise, described in the plan as the primary inference, appears only descriptively: no DM tests or Holm-adjusted p-values are reported for it, despite the plan listing multiple-testing control (Holm, Model Confidence Set; Hansen, Lunde and Nason 2011). The three-year hold-out coincides with record-warm years, a point the authors admit. *Path to revision:* cite and discuss Diebold (2015), Giacomini and White (2006) and Clark and West (2007); report rolling-origin DM tests with HAC variance; present p-values as indicative.

5. **Contribution is not positioned against any prior work.** There is no related-work section and no contribution paragraph that names a closest paper. The stated contribution ("transparent local trend estimate", "forecast gain of simple models", "calibration of intervals") is generic and untied to a gap. The authors need to show that statistical seasonal forecasting of temperature at multi-year horizons, or reanalysis-cell trend reporting for a German city, is not already covered. A concise contribution matrix against (a) DWD and regional climate reports, (b) statistical forecasting evaluations on temperature series, and (c) dynamical seasonal and decadal prediction would help. Currently a reader cannot tell what is new beyond a worked application, so the novelty score is low. The authors' own plan rated novelty and importance at 2/5, and the manuscript has not raised them.

## MINOR CONCERNS

1. **Stationarity interpretation.** The text says rejection by both ADF and KPSS "points to trend-stationary behaviour". When ADF rejects a unit root (constant only) and KPSS rejects level stationarity, the standard reading is that the series is stationary around a trend, but this requires a trend-stationary KPSS (`ct` form) and an ADF with trend, not a level KPSS. The draft reports a level KPSS, which will reject under a deterministic trend by construction. Cite the relevant literature (Kwiatkowski et al. 1992 discuss the trend-stationary variant) and rerun or reword. The conclusion should be hedged.
2. **Hamed-Rao correction is used without citation.** The paper uses an "autocorrelation-corrected variant" of Mann-Kendall but does not cite Hamed and Rao (1998). The reported variance inflation factor of exactly one deserves a comment.
3. **Bibliography mechanics.** The file ends with two `\bibliographystyle`/`\bibliography` pairs (apalike with `literature`, plainnat with `refs`). This produces duplicate or conflicting reference lists and likely compile warnings. Keep one. The title is still "Untitled Paper". Citation commands for climate sources are absent because the keys do not exist in the bib. The literature scanner explicitly said to register them first.
4. **STL and seasonal-decomposition references.** The plan includes STL (Cleveland et al. 1990) and seasonality strength measures, but the draft uses neither, so these should not appear as promised methods. Consider citing Wilks for atmospheric statistics conventions.
5. **Interval evaluation.** Coverage and width are reported, but there is no proper scoring rule reference (Gneiting and Raftery 2007; interval score). With 36 points, near-nominal coverage cannot be distinguished from 95%, as the authors acknowledge. A scoring-rule comparison would be more informative.
6. **Hypothesis wording.** H1 is described once as "marginally above" the prior range and the spec says "upper edge". The point estimate of 0.50 is at the edge of 0.3-0.5, so "supported in sign but not fully in magnitude" is overly negative. H2's MASE prior (0.75-0.95) was not met (0.48-0.50); the text says "support for H2" without noting that the magnitude prediction failed, which the specification file does note. Align the paper with the specification.
7. **Urban heat island.** The data section mentions it as a limitation without citing the literature on urban effects in Frankfurt or in reanalysis cells. The grid elevation (171.6 m) reported in the data summary is not mentioned in the paper.
8. **Practitioner claims.** The statement that practitioners "commonly" use the seasonal-naive heuristic is unsupported. Either cite evidence on industry practice or soften it.

## POSITIVE ASPECTS

1. The methods citations are accurate and well-chosen: MASE (Hyndman and Koehler 2006), Tashman (2000), DM with HLN correction, Mann-Kendall/Sen, ADF and KPSS. Their use is appropriate for the design.
2. The setup is frozen before estimation, hypotheses are stated in advance, every declared model is reported including the naive baseline, and the draft frames results as descriptive, avoiding causal language.
3. The paper is candid about the main limitations (reanalysis cell versus station, short hold-out, warm hold-out), and about the modest contribution. The rolling-origin results are used to temper the hold-out gains.
4. The literature scan was honest about which references are verified and which are not, which makes the repair path clear: the missing citations are known and can be registered.

OVERALL SCORE: 4.6/10
RECOMMENDATION: Major Revision
